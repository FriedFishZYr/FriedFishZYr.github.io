Research question

When should AI feedback ask a learner to try again, and when should it explain more?

Project overview

Context

Online calculus learners often know an answer is wrong without knowing why. MuFIN’s LLM-generated feedback narrates a visual diagnosis of the error, asks a short question about it, and offers an explanation.

Objectives

  • Compare two ways of timing the explanation (team study)
  • Find out whether a good answer inside the feedback carries over to the original problem
  • Turn the evidence into design changes and a testable next study

Notes

The MuFIN team designed and ran the randomized study. I joined in August 2026 and led the pathway analysis as an independent study. Design recommendations are proposed, not yet implemented.

The team ran the experiment. I picked up the question it left open and took it through six steps, each built on the last.

01

The team study

Two answers to the assistance dilemma

Too much help too early can cut learning short; too little can leave learners stuck (Koedinger and Aleven, 2007). The team tested two versions of the same feedback that differ only in when the full explanation appears.

Non-layered

Explain early

74 learners

The full explanation is available after every wrong attempt. It helps learners with less prior knowledge, but may cut short their own work on the error.

Layered

Try again first

64 learners

The explanation waits while learners retry, much like tiered hints in tutoring systems. Short answers unlock it after the third attempt.

One session, about 75 minutes plus screening

Measured: learning (pre/post test and two transfer items), behavior logs, the quality of embedded answers, and an experience survey.

What the team found

  • Learning

    No detectable difference in post-test or transfer scores.

  • Behavior

    Layered learners submitted more (median 17 vs. 12) but corrected fewer errors (uptake 70.9% vs. 80.5%).

  • Response quality

    In both groups, better embedded answers went with better post-test and transfer scores.

  • Experience

    No differences in engagement, cognitive load, or perceived feedback.

Adjusted for pre-test: post-test +0.33 of 6 (95% CI −0.19 to 0.85); transfer −0.08 of 2 (−0.33 to 0.18). Uptake is the share of a learner’s errors corrected on the next observed attempt. Source: project manuscript, sections 3–5 and Tables 1–3.

The question this left open

Averaged per learner, response quality predicted learning. But the moment that matters for design is smaller: right after a learner answers the feedback’s question, can they fix the original problem?

02

Pathway analysis: method

I changed the unit of analysis from learner to episode

The team study compared people. I zoomed in on moments: every time a learner got a problem wrong, how they answered the feedback question, and what they did next on the same problem. The approach follows educational data mining work on how learners process hints (Goldin, Koedinger and Aleven, 2012).

  1. 1

    Initial error

    First attempt on a problem, kept only if wrong

  2. 2

    Feedback

    Linked display and embedded question

  3. 3

    Embedded answer

    Last recorded score: 0, 1, or 2

  4. 4

    Next attempt

    Next submission on the same problem

Scope: the layered group, where learners retry before the full explanation appears. Observational, not a second experiment.

Judgment calls that shaped the data

  • Write the plan before fitting.

    I saved the analysis specification before seeing any model result.

  • Count first errors only.

    One episode per learner and problem, so repeated failures on a hard item can’t dominate.

  • Use the last score, not the best.

    An earlier lucky answer shouldn’t get credit for where the learner ended up.

  • A missing retry is not a failure.

    131 episodes without a retry are described, but kept out of the correctness model.

  • Rebuild, then re-check.

    An independent rebuild matched the episode table row by row.

  • Name what a score can’t show.

    A score is performance with support, not independent mastery.

03

Course corrections

What I got wrong, and how I fixed it

Four checks changed the analysis after the first pass. None of them overturned the main result, but each changed how much weight it can bear.

  • Two-option questions could be solved by switching

    My first model included three questions with only two answer choices, where changing a known wrong option is enough to succeed.

    What changed

    I refit without them: 309 retries from 52 learners. The full-credit signal held at +33.3 points (21.1 to 46.2).

  • An inherited claim did not reproduce

    A planning memo said 3 of the 32 mismatch cases repeated the original wrong answer, and it mixed up the two-option items with the all-correct items.

    What changed

    The raw logs show 2 of 32 (1 of 25 multiple-choice). I tested the memo’s numbers against the raw logs instead of building on them.

  • My own first report overstated a limitation

    It said the embedded prompts were missing from the data export.

    What changed

    The follow-up recovered source-linked prompts for all 92 short-answer episodes in the layered group, and I corrected the claim in writing.

  • One item’s answer key disagreed with the logs

    For one multiple-choice item, the question bank’s answer key disagreed with 207 logged attempts.

    What changed

    I withheld misconception labels for its two cases rather than guess.

04

Pathway analysis: results

A full-credit answer is a strong signal

Across 492 episodes, what a learner did inside the feedback predicted what they did next, even after accounting for prior knowledge, learner differences, and problem difficulty.

Full credit made a fix about 30 points more likely

  • 55%

    next-attempt success after an incorrect embedded answer (43 to 65)

  • 74%

    after partial credit (54 to 87, uncertain)

  • 85%

    after full credit (81 to 89)

Full credit vs. incorrect: +30.5 points (95% credible interval 18.7 to 43.0). An association, not a causal effect.

Why this model. Episodes from one learner are related, problems differ in difficulty, and partial-credit cells are small (22 retries). A Bayesian multilevel logistic model with crossed learner and problem effects, adjusted for pre-test, handles all three and reports uncertainty directly.

Adjusted probability that the next attempt on the same problem is correct, among 361 observed retries. Ranges are 95% credible intervals.

What it supports

Embedded-answer quality is a usable, in-the-moment signal of readiness to retry: the kind an adaptive system could act on, once the scorer is checked.

What it doesn’t

Raising a score is not shown to cause success, and full credit is not independent mastery. Partial credit stayed too uncertain to act on.

05

Case review

Where the signal failed

In 32 of 262 retries after a full-credit embedded answer (12.2%), the next attempt was still wrong. Averages can’t say why, so I read every case against the logged prompt, the learner’s answers, and the LearnVia question bank and its skill map.

Why averages weren’t enough

Two learners can share one label, right inside the feedback and wrong on the next try, for different reasons. One fixes the targeted error and slips elsewhere; the other keeps the error.

I made this example before the review to define what to look for. It also showed the limit: a final answer alone often can’t tell the two patterns apart.

Hypothetical example, not study data.

25 multiple-choice and 7 short-answer cases, from 24 learners and 13 problems. Mechanisms stay labeled as indeterminate where the logs cannot show the learner’s reasoning.

  3. 3 Learner’s answer

Where learners stall. In layered feedback, 23 of 38 short-answer episodes that ended with an incorrect embedded answer had no retry at all. The samples are small and descriptive, but they point to where waiting to explain may cost the most.

06

Design implications

Five changes the evidence supports

These are proposed, not implemented. Each one is tied to a specific piece of evidence, so the team can weigh it on its own.

  1. Explain sooner after a weak embedded answer

    Especially on short-answer problems.

    Evidence

    Adjusted next-attempt success: 55% after an incorrect embedded answer vs. 85% after full credit; the short-answer stall in section 05.

  2. Bridge from concept to action

    After a conceptual question, prompt the learner to apply it to the original task.

    Evidence

    Four learners named the right concept but missed the number.

  3. One grading rule per flow

    Share tolerance rules across both scorers, and reject non-answers.

    Evidence

    6.001 was accepted, then rejected; “HELP” scored full credit.

  4. Judge feedback by quality, not clicks

    Report response quality and next-attempt correction next to activity.

    Evidence

    More submissions in layered feedback came with lower uptake.

  5. Log what the next study needs

    Prompt versions, option order, answer-reveal events, and retry availability.

    Evidence

    One item’s answer key disagreed with 207 logged attempts; answer-reveal logs were mostly blank.

07

Next study

Turn the signal into a testable design decision

The evidence suggests a rule: offer a fuller explanation when the embedded answer is weak and the retry fails. That is a hypothesis, not a result. This is the study I would run to test it.

Control

Current layered rule

The explanation waits while learners keep retrying.

Treatment

Adaptive rule

Offer the explanation after a weak embedded answer and a failed retry.

Same problems, same feedback content, same logging. Only the timing of the explanation changes.

Primary outcome

Independent problem solving after a delay: learning, not in-the-moment correction.

Secondary outcomes

Next-attempt correction, response quality, time, extraneous cognitive load, perceived effort, and drop-off.

08

Limits and reflection

What the evidence still can’t tell us

Every finding here is an association inside one remote session. These are the limits worth holding onto before acting on it.

  • Causality

    Learners were randomized to a design, not to answer quality. Ability and the help received may explain part of the link.

  • Measurement

    Scores are not human-validated. Prompt versions across retries and answer-reveal timing remain unverified.

  • Scope

    One remote session and two transfer items can’t show lasting learning. A missing retry doesn’t prove disengagement.

Reflection

Audit the items before the first model. The two-option questions surfaced after the core model and changed the sample, and an answer-key mismatch limited the case coding. Next time, the item audit comes first.

A log records what the interface allowed. The same click count means different things under different designs. Reading behavior against the design that produced it was the most useful habit this project built.
